`encode.cjs` renders the two MP4s and posters in `media/`.

Uranus: three separately replayable slides (0–3.5, 3.5–9, 9–11.5 seconds),
30 fps, 1200×900. The reader supplies Previous, Next, Replay, Play/Pause,
per-slide seeking, and fullscreen controls styled like Qwilfish; Play/Pause keeps its label distinct from Replay; playback pauses at each boundary. The night sky stays on the left;
the right column holds Uranus, an illustrative Mars, and a star.

1. The blur layer fades in and out while the first requested Pokémon menu plays.
2. The telescope zooms 227×→932×. Each close-up keeps the original size on the
   left and shows the live size on the right. Blur continues pulsing in both
   the sky and the close-ups. Both planetary disks grow by 932/227; the stellar
   spot grows by an illustrative factor of 1.3.
3. A menu in the middle of the sky points to the two planets. Matching red
   LaTeX boxes emphasize 4.1\times for both planets and the telescope zoom.
   All magnification labels are rendered from LaTeX with the bundled MathJax.

Mars and the star field are illustrative, not a reconstruction of
Herschel's observation. Menu wording follows the author's requested script.
The stellar 1.3× growth is a pedagogical exaggeration, not an optical law:
seeing and diffraction blur can themselves magnify. The blur toggle is an
explanatory layer, not footage of a telescope removing atmospheric blur.
Source: Herschel's 1781 *Account of a Comet*,
[transcribed here](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Herschel_William_papers/).

Neptune: the original short-arc geodesic attempt (commit `d072ad4`), with
its title updated to the requested wording and a shared midnight-blue/violet potential colormap,
8 seconds, 30 fps, 1200×760. Both panels show **identical simulated Uranus motion**.
The purple path is the model trajectory from the same initial position/velocity:
Sun only on the left, Sun + Neptune on the right. Its present-time neighborhood
is bright. The turquoise trace records Uranus's motion. Faint path families have
45 fixed initial states, integrated once; a moving time window reveals their
coherent evolution. No curves are reseeded or fitted to the moving Uranus.

This is a schematic restricted Sun–Neptune model, not historical observed
astrometry. Units: GM_sun=1, Uranus initial radius/speed=1, Neptune orbital
radius=1.55 and initial phase=0.65 radians. Neptune's mass is 0.05 solar masses,
roughly 1,000 times its real mass, to make the separation visible. The force,
potential shading, and trajectory field all use this same exaggerated mass.
The Sun-centered frame includes the indirect acceleration from Neptune's pull
on the Sun. Jupiter, Saturn, and mutual orbital evolution are omitted.

For position r and prescribed circular Neptune position N(t):

```
Phi(t,r) = -1/|r| - m/|r-N(t)| + m r.N(t)/R^3
acceleration = -r/|r|^3 + m[(N(t)-r)/|N(t)-r|^3 - N(t)/R^3]
```

The background samples Phi (clipped in color near the Sun and Neptune); the
integrator uses the unsoftened force. No orbit is generated by following a
potential contour. Integrations use scipy's solve_ivp at rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12.
`model.py` checks the Sun-only orbit against the analytic circle and the full
orbit against a tighter-tolerance integration.

**Geodesic meaning.** These Newtonian trajectories are spatial projections of
geodesics of the time-dependent Eisenhart metric
`ds² = |dr|² + 2 dt du - 2 Phi(t,r) dt²`. They are not geodesics on the literal
height graph of Phi, and position alone does not select a trajectory: the
initial velocity matters. The highlighted model trajectories share the initial
state; the comparison does not repeatedly reset the Sun-only prediction to the
observed body. [Time-dependent Eisenhart lift, §3.1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjc/s10052-018-5789-x).

The captions explicitly mark both exaggerations. Fable authored the telescope
renderer and reviewed the visual comparison. New caption prose is marked red
for author review; existing manuscript prose is retained.
